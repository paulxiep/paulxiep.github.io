No one was incentivised to intervene, and the outcome was already overdetermined.

A company in decline has laid off its development team, and will cease operations this month.

The diagnoses were given to relevant people months before, now they became an autopsy. And now the mistakes have become shareable lessons, because the prognosis has played out to the end.

1. Presumption that an open research problem is unsolved because no one has the idea of the market gap.
2. A product that presupposes perfect automation in unbounded problem space with no human in the loop.
3. Mandate to outsource judgment and thinking to AI.
4. Penalties with zero reward for anyone who could possibly help.

(1) probably needs no explanation. The pathology lay in the prior belief and the refusal to be informed by any outside evidence.

(2). First consider a counter-case, the shape of the exception where automation is more reliable than humans - a calculator. Each calculator operation takes 1-2 of 1-dimensional input, is deterministic, and the set of types of operations is limited.

There are related concepts in number and set theories pertaining to boundedness, countability and finiteness. Calculators operate on dimensionally bounded input on a finite set of operations.

Real world problems usually operate on unbounded input space, and the operations are either ill-defined or infinitely variable. An algorithm can only encode a limited shape of logic and operations, while ML and AI are non-deterministic.

(3) stems from the belief that AI is omniscient and infallible. Most agentic systems have to undergo rigorous evaluation and guardrails before they're signed off for limited real use. Once the mandate was to have AI govern development and people, the state became terminal.

(4). While the first 3 pathologies were severe, a turnaround might have prevented the worst possible outcome. Penalising disagreements and sharing no potential upsides was what prevented any course correction from happening.

Lastly, a combination of these pathologies was what sank it. The partial solution to open research could've been a useful product, if the product had been allowed to accept human correction. Someone could've persevered and championed a fix, even solve the open research problem. But when doing so would not be appreciated or rewarded in any form, any goodwill and dedication eventually evaporated.

The outcome was already overdetermined.
